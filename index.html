Method

The pipeline

Every stage was chosen from data-quality evidence: digit-level tokenization, exact-division-only generation, backward-constructed multi-op lines, and a 20k-line automated audit (0 errors).

01 · DATA

Synthetic corpus

3M lines of compact add/subtract, 2–4 digit operands, uniform sampling, deduped. Reversed-answer variant at 50% for run 3.

math_corpus_generator.py · audited
02 · TOKENIZER

14-token digit vocab

0–9 + − = <eos> — every digit is one token so carry/borrow propagation is one decision per step. <eos> is a single terminal token.

no BPE — no subword merge

What changed

Six more findings

07
🧱

The wall moves, it never lifts

Training on 2–9 digits took 5–6 digit accuracy from 0% to 81.85% — the run-3 wall was a data gap, not a capability limit. But 10–12 digit stayed at exactly 0.00% across all four runs and a 5× token sweep. Extend the range and the wall follows it.

08
🔚

A second wall at the short end

Single-digit problems were never generated (--min-digits 2), and every model scored 0%. The cause is not arithmetic: on 1-digit answers the first emitted digit is correct 91/91 = 100%, but <eos> ranks ~10th, so it cannot stop — 1+2= → 3633. It computes perfectly and runs on.

09
📐

Filling the short end lifts everything

Adding 1-digit operands gained +14 to +22 points at every answer length, not just short ones: 2–9d 70.80→86.25%, 5–6d 59.25→81.85%, 2–4d 47.50→61.90%. The cheapest single change in the whole study.

10
⚠️

Never select checkpoints by loss

In run 6, ckpt_final beat the loss-selected ckpt_best by 8–10 points on every split while being 0.0005 worse on eval loss — noise, in a curve flat to ±0.005 for its last 2000 steps. Loss and accuracy diverge here; loss-based selection picks at random.

11
⚖️

More tokens are not uniformly better

100 tpp beat 20 tpp on 5–6 digit (80.85 vs 59.25%) but lost ground on 2–4 digit (34.85 vs 47.50%). At 5.1M params the model reallocates capacity toward wherever the data mass sits. This is a capacity ceiling, not a data problem.

12
🚀

The trainer was 7× too slow

torch.compile was disabled on a wrong assumption, and every step shuffled a 258k-element list to pick 128. Fixed: 53.7k → 372k tok/s, a 20-tpp run in 276s. Caveat: the 14-token vocab is itself worth 1.53× versus a 16k-vocab reference — at dim 256 the lm_head is 45% of forward FLOPs at V=16,384 but 0.07% at V=14.

What run 8 showed

Three more findings

13
✖️

Multiplication is a different problem

5.20% versus 60–83% for the other three ops. And it is not the length wall: mul gets the length right 54% of the time but the first digit right only 12% — it knows how big the answer is and cannot compute it. 9x9 → 1011. Addition is digit-local with a carry; multiplication needs every partial product at once. A 5M digit-level decoder does not do that.

14
➗

Exact division is easier than multiplication

60.53% — close to addition, nowhere near mul. The generator builds division backward (a = q·d, d in 2–9), so recovering q is closer to a digit-local scan than to a full product. The corpus construction, not the operation, sets the difficulty.

15
🕳️

A held-out set can be contaminated by construction

The 10–12 digit test scored 9.67% — apparently the first crack in the wall. It was an artifact: --min-digits 10 bounds the dividend, but division is built as a = q·d with small d, so quotients land back inside the trained range. Restricted to genuine 10–12 digit add/sub, the score is 0.00%. The wall stands across five runs.

Runs 9–10

The short-digit wall was a formatting bug

Run 9 narrowed the corpus to 1–4 digits at 100 tpp. It scored 60.15% on the original 2–4 digit eval — still below run 3's 78.70%, with 2-digit answers at 1%. The digits were right; the model would not stop. 40 seconds of SFT — 0.114% of the pretraining budget — fixed it.

What SFT showed

Three final findings

16
🛑

Packed pretraining never teaches stopping

Loss over a packed stream makes <eos> one token among millions, never the target of a distinct example. Masking the prompt to -100 and supervising only the completion — the recipe from llm-modern-arch-experiment, whose SFT exists for this exact reason — took the 2–4 digit eval from 60.15% to 99.05% in 1000 updates. That finally beats run 3's 78.70%, by 20 points.

17
🔀

SFT also collapsed the reversed/forward ambiguity

Strict (forward-order-only) accuracy went 21.55% → 99.05%, exactly matching lenient. Every earlier run leaned on a scorer that accepted either order, because 50/50 reversed training left the model emitting a mix. After SFT the two metrics are identical: it emits forward order every time. 7+5= → 21 became 12.

18
🧱

SFT fixes format, not capability

5–6 digit stayed at exactly 0.00% before and after. Supervising the completion teaches when to stop and which order to emit; it cannot teach arithmetic the model never learned. That cleanly separates the two walls: the short-side one was a formatting artifact and is gone, the long-side one is a real capacity limit and is untouched.

What run 11 showed

Two corrections

19
🔁

Multiplication's failure was partly formatting after all

The pre-SFT diagnosis — length right 54%, first digit right 12% — read as a pure computation failure. SFT moved those to 99.0% and 95.7%. Small products are now perfect (9x9=81, 12x11=132, 100% at 1–2 digit answers). The prediction that SFT would leave mul near 5% was wrong.

20
📉

What remains is a clean size gradient

Post-SFT multiplication by answer length: 2-digit 100%, 4-digit 70%, 7-digit 36%, 10-digit 15%, 11-digit 2%. It fails in the middle digits of long products (345x67= → 22415, gold 23115). That residual is genuine capacity for carrying partial products, and output supervision cannot touch it.

What runs 12–13 showed

Three more findings

21
📚

The binding constraint is corpus diversity

The 1–4 digit four-op space is ~10⁸ problems; a 38M-line corpus exhausts it. Run 12 saturated at step 1600 of 15600 — ~10 tpp effective, not 100 — then memorized. The 1–6 digit space is ~2×10¹², six orders larger, and the same recipe at a fifth of the nominal budget beat it everywhere. Budget only helps while there is novel content to spend it on.

22
📐

Multiplication degrades smoothly with product length

Run 13 post-SFT by answer length: 4-digit 79%, 5-digit 77%, 6-digit 60%, 7-digit 41%, 8-digit 8%. A gradient, not a cliff — consistent with a capacity limit on carrying partial products. Run 12's apparent 0.40% collapse was an artifact: saturation forced its held-out set entirely into the 7–8 digit band where mul is weakest.

23
🚪

The first crack in the length wall

Every run through 12 scored exactly 0.00% outside its training range. Run 13 scores 12.27% on unseen 7–8 digit operands — 81% where the answer is 5 digits, 74% at 6 digits, 0% at 8. It does not extrapolate to longer answers, but it now handles longer operands when the answer stays in a familiar range.

Run 14

Adding exponentiation

Five operations at 1–6 digits, 100 tpp, then SFT. The generator's pow was widened from a hardcoded base 2–9 / exp 2–4 (only 24 distinct problems) to uniform sampling over every (base, exp) pair whose answer fits the digit budget — 21,533 problems, 9,998 bases, exponents 2–39. That made pow a real generalization test instead of a lookup table.

What run 14 showed

Three closing findings

24
📏

Pow's 20% is answer length, not exponentiation

Held-out pow by answer length: 3-digit 100%, 6-digit 73%, 7-digit 55%, 8-digit 13%, 12-digit 1%. 68% of held-out pow problems have 8+ digit answers — the band where multiplication is already at 19%. Where the answer is short, exponentiation works as well as anything else. Same length curve, different operation.

25
🔍

Held-out and memorized converge — so it is not a lookup

Comparing held-out pow against the training portion: at 8+ digits they land on the same near-zero values (13% vs 16%, 1% vs 2%, 1% vs 4%). A memorizing model would keep the training portion high. It does not — the same length-limited computation runs on both, which is what makes this learned rather than stored.

26
🎯

2^32 fails the way multiplication fails

2^32 → 4144961696, gold 4294967296: right length, right leading digit, wrong middle — the exact multiplication signature. Meanwhile 2^10, 2^16, 3^5, 7^3 are all correct. Seen 84 times in training and still wrong, because the failure is carrying digits, not recall.

What run 15 showed

Composition is not the barrier

27
🧩

Multi-op fails on digits, not on structure

Compositional answers: 91.2% correct length, 83.0% correct first digit, 15.27% exact. 189+(532132-432349)*6 → 588003 (gold 598887) — right magnitude, right leading digits, wrong middle. (818-(1463-645))*7=0 is exactly right, nested subtraction and all. The model resolves parentheses and applies precedence correctly; it cannot carry digits through a long result. The by-length curve mirrors multiplication exactly.

28
🪤

A saturated corpus can invert a held-out set

Run 15's pow scored 0.00% — but the 38M-line corpus had absorbed every short-answer pow problem, leaving a held-out set containing only 7–12 digit answers and none of the 1–6 digit range where run 14 scored 73–100%. The metric measured pow exclusively in the band where every operation is near zero. Not a regression; a broken comparison.

What run 16 showed

Encoding is free; carrying is not

29
🔤

A 13-token phrase is as good as one symbol

Every operation lands within ~2 points across encodings, and subtraction is identical to two decimals. The model learned that multiplied by and x denote the same operation rather than memorising two disjoint tasks. Multiplication is slightly better in word form. Operator identity survives being spread across many tokens.

30
🔗

Both encodings fail identically

7 ^ 3 → 327 and 7 to the power of 3 → 327: the same wrong digits, from the same model, on the same problem written two ways. Shared failure is stronger evidence than shared success — parallel lookup tables would diverge. Meanwhile mul (33–36%) and multi-op (16.25%) sit exactly where they have all study, with the same by-length collapse. Encoding, composition, operation count and vocabulary size all leave the digit-carrying ceiling untouched.

What run 17 showed

Three findings

31
📈

Budget finally moved multiplication

33.33% → 40.25%, the first time more compute has shifted mul in the whole study. The length curve extended with it: 86% at 6-digit answers, 71% at 7-digit, and it now reaches 11-digit answers at all. Doubling tokens and widening digits bought real capability on single operations.

32
🪆

Wider digits help operations and hurt composition

Multi-op fell 16.25% → 5.08%. Re-scored against run 16's exact answer-length profile it stays at 5.08%, so it is not an eval artifact; scored per template the drop is uniform — the old N*(N+N)-N scores 0.0%, same as the new shapes, so the added templates are not the cause. At 1–8 digits the intermediate values inside a compositional expression grow, and every sub-result must be carried correctly.

33
✳️

An accidental operator-transfer experiment

--mul-symbol governed only single-op lines; build_multi hardcoded *. So the corpus rendered multiply four ways and no line ever contained both x and *. On 400 identical problems: x 41.75%, times 41.75%, multiplied by 37.00%, * 22.00%. The model inferred * means multiply from compositional context alone — well above chance — but 20 points worse than the form it saw standalone. Generator now fixed; the reported mul figures are x-only.

Corrections

Two claims that did not survive checking

34
🔣

Operator consistency is worth 30 points

The same symbol scored 22.00% when it appeared only inside compositional expressions and 52.00% when it also appeared as a standalone binary operator. Same model size, same budget, same digit range. A rendering inconsistency invisible in the corpus statistics cost more than any architecture or curriculum change tried in this study.

35
✂️

The mul cap limits how balanced a product can be

build_single caps mul answers at max_d + 2. A d×d product needs ~2d digits, so the widest balanced product is about max_d/2 + 1: a 1–6 digit corpus tops out at 4×4 and contains no 5*5 despite having 6-digit operands, while 1–8 does reach 5×5 and 1–16 reaches 9×9. Wide lopsided products (6×2, 5×1) slip under the cap at any width. Forcing min=max≥4 collapses the generator to 81 single-digit lines entirely.

36
🧮

Division does not generalize — the bucketing was wrong

Division appeared to survive out-of-range at 62.62% where add/sub scored 0.00%. It was an artifact: the eval was bucketed by dividend width, but a = q·d emits dividends across the whole range, so 43% of that set had quotients of ≤8 digits. Scored by quotient length: 1–8 digits 100%, 9 digits 13.2%, 10 digits 1.2%, 11–12 digits 0.0%. All four operations collapse at the same answer-length boundary — a cleaner result than the exception first reported.

The central correction

Two constraints, not one

37
⚖️

Balance binds before length does

At identical answer lengths, lopsided multiplication works and balanced multiplication does not: a 12-digit answer scores 84.2% when one operand is 1–2 digits and 0.0% when both are 3+. Same output size, same model, same corpus. 84729 * 7 is one pass with a single carry chain; 847 * 293 needs three partial products held and summed with alignment. The model does the first and cannot do the second at all.

38
🔁

Earlier mul numbers tracked corpus composition

This reframes every multiplication figure in the study. Run 18 scored 52.00% against run 17's 40.25% — but run 18's 1–6 digit corpus cannot produce anything past 4×4, so its eval was dominated by lopsided pairs. The metric moved because the corpus changed shape, not because the model improved. Reported mul accuracy is only meaningful alongside the balance distribution of its eval set.

What run 21 showed

Three findings

39
🎚️

The SFT masking was the real culprit

Run 20's traces died under SFT (30.83% → 0.00%) and the cause was mechanical, not conceptual: load_sft split the prompt on the last =, so a trace's entire chain sat inside the prompt and only the final number was supervised — SFT was training the model to skip its own reasoning. Supervising from the first control token onward covers 32 of 44 tokens instead of ~6.

40
➕

The residual failure is the final sum, not the decomposition

On traced problems the model gets 99.0% of partial products right but only 60.0% of the closing addition — and final-answer accuracy (59.7%) tracks the sum, not the products. 6014 * 66 yields 360840 and 36084 correctly, states the sum, then writes 496924 for 396924. Decomposition solved the multiplication; a long multi-term addition is now the bottleneck.

41
🚦

<think> is routing, not free capability

Asking for a chain where none applies is actively harmful: add falls 86.60% → 0.40% and div 100% → 0.80% under <think>, because those operations have no trace format in training and the model emits something malformed. The switch decides when to reason; it does not make reasoning universally better.

What run 22 showed

Two findings

42
🔢

The bottleneck is chain length, not arithmetic

Partial-product traces stayed at 62.00% despite the closing-sum fix. Broken out by how many partial products the problem requires: 2 products 97.5%, 3 products 40.7% — and at 3 the model emits the right number of steps only 48.4% of the time. 50949 * 224 produces the ×200 and ×20 terms, skips ×4, sums what it has, and returns a consistent but wrong answer. Per-step accuracy is 99.6% for products and 99.2% for adds: the steps it writes are right, it writes too few.

43
↔️

A trace can transfer to the direct capability

Column-wise carry traces took direct wide addition to 99.40% — no <think> required. Run 21 measured the same operation at 44–80% depending on width. This is the first case in the study where training on a decomposition improved the one-shot skill rather than only the traced form, which suggests the trace taught column structure rather than a surface format.

What run 23 showed

The symptom was not the cause

44
🎯

Chain length was a symptom of capacity, not a control failure

The intervention worked on its own terms — the model declares the correct step count 93.2% of the time, up from 48.4%. And the answer got worse. Forced to commit to three steps, it emits three steps with wrong content instead of two steps and a wrong sum. Dropping a term was never a counting problem; it was what running out of capacity looked like. Two partial products is the ceiling — 2 products reached 100.0%, and no output scaffolding moved the third.

Two findings

45
🪶

Format is cheap; arithmetic is not

The 1M model holds reasoning structure almost perfectly — tr_reduce chain fidelity 99.25% against 99.75%, and tr_partial loses only 5.25 points. What the extra four million parameters buy is accuracy inside the steps: add −18.50, sub −14.50, algebra −19.00. Learning to emit a valid chain and learning to compute correctly within it are separable capabilities with very different parameter costs.

46
🎁

Division was never using the parameters

100.00% at both scales, every answer width from 4 to 16 digits. The generator builds division backward (a = q·d with small d), so recovering the quotient is a pattern-match rather than a carry-propagating computation. Four fifths of the model was buying nothing there — and multiplication lost only 0.50 points because at 6% it had nothing to lose.

What we learned

Four findings

Loss decomposition: first answer digit costs 2.44 nats (near-uniform over 14), the rest 1.23 (ln 14 = 2.64). That single number explains everything below.

01
⏹

<eos> stopping is free & robust

100% stop rate in all three runs, all lengths. The terminal-token objective is the cheapest capability in the whole corpus — it generalizes perfectly.

02
🔁

Reversed answers: +12.5 pts, zero cost

Training 50% of lines with reversed answer digits aligns left-to-right decoding with right-to-left carry propagation. 66.15% → 78.70% exact-match. Keep it.

03
🧱

Length generalization is the wall

0% on 5–6 digit inputs in every run, at 10× params of tokens. A 5M causal decoder learned digit-level statistics, not column-wise arithmetic. The attention pattern carry propagation needs (digit attending to its right-hand neighbor) fights the decoding direction.

04
⚡

Modern stack + Muon is the right tool

704s vs 958s at 2× data, flat loss curve to step 2000 (no overfit) vs run 1's overfit after ~epoch 18. bf16 + 32K tokens/update saturated the GPU at 96%.

05
📏

Failures are boundary errors

Run 3's mistakes are almost all length/termination errors — right digits, wrong count. The model knows the arithmetic; it doesn't know when the number ends.

06
🎯

Good data ≠ more data

Run 1 proved the budget principle: 33.5M tokens of an overfit fp32 run ≈ 66M tokens of a well-optimized one. Curation and recipe beat raw volume at this scale.
