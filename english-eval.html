English model

The arithmetic side of this study scores exact string match on the answer after =. English has no answer and no unique correct continuation, so that scorer reports 0% on a perfect model. Three evaluators, built against the generator's own grammar, replace it — and they disagree with each other in a way that turns out to be the finding.

What the model is

A 274K-parameter causal decoder — dim 64, 5 layers, 4 heads at head_dim=16 — trained character-level on synthetic English from synth_word.py, a dependency-free generator that builds sentences from part-of-speech word banks and a small clause grammar. No LLM in the loop. 655 word types, 3.7M unique sentences, 308M tokens.

Two checkpoints are compared: 100 tpp (815 steps, 29 seconds) and 1000 tpp (8,149 steps, 180 seconds, 0.866 epochs — every sequence seen at most once). Same architecture, same generator, 10× the compute.

These evaluators measure conformance to synth_word.py's grammar, not English. A model that perfectly memorized the generator's quirks would score 100%. That is the right measure for "did this architecture learn its training distribution" and the wrong one for "does it know English." Read every number below with that framing.

1Grammatical well-formedness

Generate 500 sentences unprompted at temperature 0.8, then parse each one against the generator's own word banks. Six checks run in order of how cheaply they fail: lexicon (is every token a real word form), det_num, agree, aux, neg, and subj.

Calibrating the checker first

A checker is only as good as its false-positive rate, so it was run against 19,191 lines of known-good generator output — text that is correct by construction. The first version scored 84.70%, which meant a 15.3% false-positive rate: the checker was wrong, not the generator. Four bugs came out of that:

2Next-word admissibility

Take a held-out sentence, cut it at a random word boundary, and greedy-decode the next word. Two scores: exact (did it produce the same word the corpus did) and admissible (is the word it produced grammatically legal in that slot, by part-of-speech class).

Exact match is the wrong question here — after "The teacher " there are hundreds of valid continuations, so a perfect model still scores low. Admissibility is the real measure. The permitted set is a median of 86 words against a 482-word lexicon, so this is roughly a 1-in-6 baseline, not a coin flip.

Metric100 tpp1000 tppChange
Exact word match6.82%7.07%+0.25
Admissible class69.70%69.19%−0.51

This metric did not move across a 10× budget increase. Both numbers sit inside noise on n=396. That is not a broken evaluator — the admissible set is genuinely selective — it is a real result, and it is the most interesting one on this page.

The reading: local part-of-speech prediction saturates early. Knowing that a determiner is followed by an adjective or a noun is a short-range regularity the model has essentially learned by 100 tpp. What the extra compute bought was spelling those words correctly and holding structure across a whole sentence — neither of which this metric can see, because it only ever looks one word ahead.

3Minimal pairs

BLiMP-style forced choice. Build 960 sentence pairs identical but for one grammatical feature, score each member by length-normalized per-token negative log-likelihood, and check whether the model assigns higher likelihood to the grammatical one. Chance is 50%.

The pair set is validated independently: 98.8% of the grammatical members pass the eval-1 checker and only 1.9% of the ungrammatical ones do, so the two evaluators agree on which member is which.

Already saturated at 100 tpp. After 29 seconds of training, a 274K model prefers the grammatical member of a minimal pair 98.23% of the time — and 10× the compute moves that by half a point. Agreement, auxiliary selection, modal complements and negation are all essentially solved before the model can reliably spell.

The one phenomenon that never reaches 100% is det_num, flat at 96.67% across both budgets. Determiner–noun number agreement is the only contrast here that requires the model to hold a feature across an intervening adjective.

Three evaluators, three different answers

The point of building all three is that they disagree, and the disagreement localizes what the extra compute actually bought.

Grammar is cheap, spelling is expensive

The model knows the dogs run beats the dogs runs at 98% after 29 seconds — while still emitting taall and theyfore. Structural knowledge arrives an order of magnitude before orthographic reliability.

A single metric would have misled

Eval 3 alone says the model was nearly finished at 100 tpp. Eval 1 alone says it improved 33 points. Both are true measurements of different things, and the loss silently averages them into one number that explains neither.

Calibrate the evaluator, not just the model

The checker scored 84.70% on text that was correct by construction. Four bugs later it reads 98.60%. Every number on this page would have been wrong by ~14 points without that step.

What is still broken is structural

The remaining 5.2% of malformed generations are mostly dropped subjects after subordinators, not misspellings — the failure mode moved up a level rather than disappearing.

Limits worth stating. All three evaluators score against synth_word.py's grammar, so none is a language benchmark. Eval 2 runs on n=396 and eval 1 on n=500 generations, so differences under ~2 points are not resolvable. The two checkpoints trained on separately generated corpora — same generator and settings, different seed — so their losses are comparable to each other but not to the arithmetic runs, which use a 55-token vocab and a different task entirely.
